Democracy · Campaign Finance

What money buys.

The contractor loophole we left open for twenty years, why a pledge is not a reform, and the one approach to Citizens United that has never been tried.

Rep. Ikaika Lardizabal Hussey  ·  State House District 29  ·  August 2026
20 years the contractor contribution loophole has stayed open
$100 the Our Hawaiʻi Pledge limit on corporate PAC and lobbyist money
0 states have tried redefining corporate powers — yet

In February 2022, two former members of the Legislature I now serve in stood in federal court and admitted taking bribes — envelopes of cash, casino chips, hotel rooms — from a wastewater contractor who wanted their help steering public work his way.1 What shocked people was not that it happened. It was how cheap it was. The price of a committee chair’s loyalty turned out to be a few thousand dollars at a time.

The felonies were prosecuted, and the two men went to prison. But the felonies were never the main event. The main event is everything around them that was, and remains, perfectly legal — the ordinary, disclosed, above-board flow of money from people who want things from government to the people who decide whether they get them. Bribery is the crude retail version of a wholesale system. If we only fix the crimes, we keep the system.

The loophole we chose to keep

Start with the piece of the system we have known about, talked about, and preserved for twenty years.

Since 2005, Hawaiʻi law has barred government contractors from making campaign contributions while their contracts are running.2 It sounds airtight. It stops at the company’s front door. The ban binds the corporate entity — and not the company’s owner, not its officers, not their spouses or their children, all of whom may give freely, and do. At fundraiser after fundraiser, the executives of firms holding public contracts hand over checks that the law pretends have nothing to do with the firm. The contractor at the center of the 2022 bribery cases had, for years, also been a prolific and entirely legal donor through exactly this channel.

This is not a hidden defect. The blue-ribbon Commission to Improve Standards of Conduct — convened by the House in direct response to those federal cases — recommended closing it.3 Bills to close it get introduced session after session. They get hearings. They die quietly, without fingerprints, the way bills die when a majority privately prefers the status quo it publicly deplores. Twenty years is not an oversight. Twenty years is a choice.

Closing it is not conceptually hard: attribute the contributions of a contractor’s owners, officers, and household members to the contractor for the duration of the contract, plus a cooling-off period. Draft it, pass it, done.

What money actually buys

Be precise about the transaction, because its defenders rely on our imprecision. Campaign money almost never buys a vote — that is the felony version, and it is genuinely rare. What money buys is upstream of any vote and mostly invisible to the public.

It buys access: the returned phone call, the meeting that fits the calendar, the amendment considered because its author asked a friend. It buys agenda: which bills get introduced, which get a hearing before a deadline, which die unheard — the most consequential power in any legislature and the one no vote ever records. And it buys deference: the benefit of the doubt, extended a hundred small times a session, that accumulates into policy.

A contractor’s executives who bundle a few thousand dollars are not buying a vote. They are buying the standing to be heard first, and the system is working precisely as designed when they get it. That is why disclosure alone — sunlight — has never been sufficient. We have disclosed this system in detail for decades. Disclosure tells you who bought the access. It does not repossess it.

A pledge is not a reform

Into this gap comes the Our Hawaiʻi Pledge: candidates promise to accept no more than $100 from corporate PACs and lobbyists.4 I keep my own fundraising consistent with it, and I understand its purpose — a pledge is how a movement makes a norm visible, and norms matter.

But I want to be honest with you about what a pledge is not. It is not a reform, for three reasons.

First, it is voluntary, which means it binds exactly the people who least need binding and leaves everyone else untouched. Second, it is unenforceable — its only sanction is embarrassment, and embarrassment has never once repealed a business model. Third, and most dangerous: a pledge can become a substitute for the statute it gestures at — a lawn sign planted where a law should stand. The donor class will happily concede the symbolism of a hundred pledges to avoid the substance of one binding act.

Movements signal with pledges. Legislatures owe the people statutes. I signed the norm; my job is to make it law, for everyone, with penalties — so that clean fundraising stops being a boutique virtue and becomes the mandatory floor.

What reform looks like

Four pieces, all drafted, all achievable, none requiring a constitutional convention:

  • Close the contractor loophole. Attribution of owner, officer, and household contributions to the contracting entity, for the contract term plus a cooling-off period. This is the Foley Commission’s own recommendation, four years stale.
  • Comprehensive public financing. A full public-funding option for every office — the reform that changes who can afford to run, not just how incumbents fundraise. A comprehensive bill has already passed one chamber of this Legislature only to die in the other;5 the design work is done, and only the will is missing.
  • Real-time disclosure. Contributions reported within days, in machine-readable form, all cycle long — not in lumps that arrive after the decisions they influenced.
  • An enforcer with teeth. The Campaign Spending Commission polices all of it on a budget that would embarrass a mid-sized nonprofit.6 Reform on paper without enforcement funding is theater.

The Citizens United argument no one has tried

Now the longer game — because the deepest constraint on all of this is a case decided five thousand miles away.

Citizens United v. FEC (2010) held that the First Amendment forbids government from restricting corporations’ independent political spending.7 For fifteen years, reformers have responded on the Court’s chosen terrain: constitutional amendments that will not pass, disclosure laws that reach only the edges, and hope for a different bench. All three may be worth pursuing. None is a plan.

But there is a door no state has walked through. A corporation is not found in nature. It is a legal instrument — chartered by a state, holding exactly those powers the state’s corporation code grants it and no others. Hawaiʻi writes that code: chapter 414 of our statutes defines what a corporation formed here is and what it may do.8 Citizens United says the government may not silence a speaker. It has never answered a prior question: whether the state must construct that speaker — must include unlimited political spending in the bundle of powers a business charter confers in the first place. A corporation exercising a power it was never granted is not being censored. It is acting ultra vires — beyond its charter — the oldest concept in corporate law.

Would redefining corporate powers this way survive the courts? Genuinely unknown — which is the point. Zero states have tried it.9 Fifteen years of complaining about Citizens United, and the one argument built from the states’ own undisputed authority — the power to define what they charter — has never been placed before a judge. Someone has to go first, with a carefully drafted statute and a clean test case. A small state with a documented corruption problem and a legislature willing to be honest about it is the right plaintiff. I intend for Hawaiʻi to be that state, and I am working with constitutional and corporate-law scholars to draft it properly rather than symbolically.

Before November

One more thing, because timing is substance. The contractor loophole could be closed before the general election. The Legislature can call itself into special session — it takes two-thirds of each chamber10 — and a one-bill session enacting the Foley Commission’s recommendation would take days, not weeks. Every incumbent on the November ballot would then face voters under the new rule instead of promising it perennially.

So here is my question for you, and I mean it as a real question, not a rhetorical one: should we call that session? Text me and tell me. The answer I hear from Kalihi will be the answer I carry into the caucus room — which is, after all, the way this is supposed to work: the access that money buys, available to you for the price of a text message.
