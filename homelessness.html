Housing · Health · Land Justice

Homelessness is not one problem.

Four populations wear the same word. An accounting of the 2026 count, what Finland actually did after 2008, and what our own kauhale program costs per outcome.

Rep. Ikaika Lardizabal Hussey  ·  State House District 29  ·  August 2026
4,539 people counted homeless on Oʻahu on a single January night
43% of the count is Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander — about 10% of residents
29,365 applicants on the Hawaiian home lands waitlist

On a single night this past January, volunteers walked Oʻahu’s streets, parks, beaches, and shelters and counted 4,539 people with no home.1 The Point-in-Time Count is our annual ritual of quantified conscience: we count, we release the number, we argue about whether it went up or down, and by spring we have mostly stopped talking about it. Everyone who works in this field will tell you the count is an undercount — it misses people sleeping in cars on private lots, families doubled up on couches, anyone who did not want to be found that night. Treat 4,539 as a floor, not a fact.

But the deeper problem with the number is not its precision. It is that a single number implies a single problem, and homelessness is not one problem. It is at least four, wearing one word. Policies fail here — expensively, repeatedly, decade after decade — because we keep prescribing one medicine for four different diagnoses.

Four problems wearing one word

The first population is the priced out. Working families, kūpuna on fixed incomes, a mother with two jobs whose landlord sold the building. Nothing is “wrong” with these neighbors except arithmetic: a market two-bedroom in urban Honolulu consumes most of a full-time minimum-wage paycheck before food, medicine, or the bus pass. When they lose housing, they are usually invisible — cars, garages, shelters, a cousin’s floor. What they need is not case management or treatment. They need money and units: shallow rent subsidies, eviction diversion, and homes that cost what our wages can pay. For this group, the cheapest exit from homelessness is never entering it — every eviction prevented for a few thousand dollars saves a shelter stay that costs many times that.

The second population lives with serious psychiatric illness. They are a minority of the count but the majority of what the public sees and fears — the man shouting at traffic on North King Street, the woman who will not leave the doorway. Vouchers alone do nothing for them; many cannot hold a lease without support, and some are too sick to accept help as our law currently offers it. They need permanent supportive housing with clinical staff in the building, and a working system of assisted community treatment for the small number who will die on the sidewalk waiting to want help.2

The third population is in active addiction — on this island, above all, methamphetamine. Addiction overlaps the other categories but obeys its own logic. The intervention that works is treatment available at the moment a person asks for it — that day, not after a six-week waitlist, not after an arrest. We fund treatment as if relapse were a moral failure rather than a symptom, and then spend triple the money on the jail cell and the emergency room that inevitability produces.

The fourth population is the dispossessed — and this is the one our national models have no category for. Native Hawaiians and Pacific Islanders are about 43 percent of Oʻahu’s homeless count, in a state where they are roughly ten percent of residents.3 That ratio is not a mystery to be studied. It is the visible end of a century of dispossession — and of a specific, quantifiable broken promise: 29,365 applicants are waiting for Hawaiian home lands that Congress set aside in 1921, and applicants regularly die waiting.4 A homelessness policy that ignores the waitlist is bailing the canoe while the leak widens. Land justice is homelessness policy in Hawaiʻi, and any accounting that omits it is cooked.

What Finland actually did

Finland is the fashionable citation in homelessness debates, so let us be precise about what it actually did — because the lesson is not the slogan.

In 2008, Finland adopted a national program (the first was nicknamed “Paavo”) built on Housing First: the principle that a person gets a permanent home first, unconditionally, and services second — rather than earning housing by graduating through shelters and sobriety requirements.5 But the philosophy was the smaller half of the program. The operative half was real estate. The Finnish state, its cities, and the nonprofit Y-Foundation bought and built thousands of apartments, and — this is the radical part — converted their shelters into permanent housing with ordinary leases. Tenants pay rent, drawn from wages or benefits. Support staff work in the building. A shelter bed became a front-door key.

The results: long-term homelessness fell by well over half, and Finland became the only country in the European Union where homelessness consistently declined year after year.6 Three lessons travel across the ocean intact. First, units precede outcomes — no philosophy houses anyone without keys attached. Second, a lease is not a program bed: permanence is what makes recovery possible, not a reward for it. Third, Finland sustained the commitment across four governments of different parties. Pilots don’t end homelessness. Permanence does.

The kauhale experiment, honestly

Hawaiʻi’s signature initiative is the kauhale — villages of small homes with shared kitchens and gathering space, some paired with medical respite for people cycling out of hospital beds.7 I want to be fair to it, because there is real merit here: kauhale stand up fast, they rebuild the social fabric that congregate shelters shred, and the medical-respite villages interrupt the most obscene cost spiral we have — the ambulance-to-ER-to-sidewalk loop.

The administration’s favorite statistic is that a kauhale bed costs a small fraction of a hospital bed or a jail cell per night. On its own narrow terms, that math is right, and moving a person from an emergency room hallway to a kauhale is a genuine gain for the person and the treasury alike.

Cost per outcome

But a cheap bed is not the goal. A home is the goal, and here our accounting goes quiet. The number that would tell us whether kauhale are working is cost per permanent exit: of the people who enter a kauhale, how many move into permanent housing and are still housed a year later, and what did each durable exit cost? That figure is not published consistently, village by village, and until it is, we are steering by anecdote.8

The risk is not that kauhale fail. The risk is that they succeed at the wrong thing — that we build a parallel village system, call it housing, and quietly park people there, at low cost per night, forever. Finland’s insight cuts here too: they closed shelters into housing; the danger is that we build shelters and rename them. A kauhale bed that circulates people back to the sidewalk is a cheap way to stand still.

So my position is not anti-kauhale. It is: publish the exits. Report cost per permanent exit for every village, every program, every contract — kauhale, shelters, outreach, all of it — and then move money toward whatever the data says actually ends homelessness for each of the four populations. Measurement is not hostility. Measurement is how you scale the things that work.

What I will do

Four problems, four responses — plus the accounting to keep us honest:

  • For the priced out: eviction diversion and shallow rent subsidies funded at scale, and the lawful housing supply I have written about elsewhere in these notes — because prevention is the cheapest program we will ever run.
  • For the sick: permanent supportive housing with clinical staffing baked into the operating budget, and a functioning assisted community treatment pathway, so the sickest are not left to the police by default.
  • For the addicted: treatment on demand — same-day beds, funded like the emergency service it is.
  • For the dispossessed: Hawaiian home lands funding as a recurring obligation, not a once-a-generation gesture. The Legislature’s $600 million appropriation in 2022 was the largest since the Act passed9 — and it will house only a fraction of the waitlist. Sustained annual appropriations, and infrastructure dollars to turn raw land into lots, are the honest price of the promise.
  • For all of it: an outcomes statute — every state-funded homelessness program reports cost per permanent exit, published annually, in one table anyone can read.

The count will come again next January. The test is not whether the number embarrasses us — it will. The test is whether, for the first time, we can say which of the four problems we shrank, by how much, for whom, and at what cost. That is what taking 4,539 people seriously actually looks like.
