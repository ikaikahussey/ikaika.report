Housing · Zoning · Enforcement

Monster homes and the density we refuse to build.

Monster homes are the form density takes when lawful density is unavailable. The remedy runs through professional licensing, disgorgement of illegal rents, and infrastructure paired with upzoning — not another daily fine.

Rep. Ikaika Lardizabal Hussey  ·  State House District 29  ·  August 2026
$250 per day — the penalty in force for years, capped at $2,000
78% of monster-home plans traced to ten developers in the 2019 audit
4th revocation under the ordinance — the Hanai Loop permit

You have seen one, even if you never learned its name. A lot in Kalihi that held a two-bedroom plantation cottage for seventy years gets scraped clean. What rises in its place fills nearly every buildable inch of the parcel: three stories, a dozen bedrooms, a rank of water heaters along the side wall, electric meters lined up like mailboxes. The neighbors count the cars — eight, ten, fourteen — and watch them colonize the street. Then they call the City. And the City, for most of the past decade, has shrugged.

We call them monster homes, and the name captures the feeling while hiding the mechanics. A monster home is not really a home at all. It is an apartment building wearing a single-family costume — built without the fire separations, the parking, the sewer-capacity review, or the inspections that an honest apartment building would be required to provide. The costume is the whole point: it lets a developer put fifteen rentable bedrooms on land where the law says only one family may live, and it lets them do it at single-family construction cost.

I want to make two arguments about monster homes. They pull in opposite directions, and both are true. The first is that this is lawbreaking for profit, and our enforcement against it has been an embarrassment. The second is that monster homes are the form density takes when lawful density is unavailable. Until we fix both halves of that sentence — the impunity and the scarcity — we will keep getting more of them.

An apartment building in costume

Most of Oʻahu’s residential land is zoned for one detached house per lot. The zoning code polices the form of the building — setbacks, height, lot coverage — but for decades it barely policed intensity: how many people, how many kitchens, how many de facto units a “house” actually contains. Monster-home developers drove a fleet of rent rolls through that gap. Plans were drawn and stamped showing a large family residence; what got built and operated was a boarding house, floor by floor, bedroom by bedroom, each door with its own lock and its own tenant.

The City Council responded with Bill 79 in 2018, capping floor-area ratios and tying bedroom counts to on-site parking.1 It slowed the crudest projects. But an ordinance is only as strong as the department enforcing it, and the Department of Planning and Permitting spent the following years demonstrating exactly how weak that could be — including permitting employees convicted in federal court of taking bribes to move plans through review.2

The demand is real

Before we talk about enforcement, be honest about who lives inside these buildings. Not the developers — they live elsewhere. The tenants are kūpuna on fixed incomes, immigrant families working two and three jobs, students, single men who would otherwise be in a car or a shelter. They pay real rent, often below the advertised market, for a bedroom and a shared kitchen.

The tenants are not the villains here. They are the evidence. Evidence that thousands of our neighbors will accept a bedroom in an illegal rooming house because the legal housing market has nothing to offer them at a price connected to local wages. Kalihi has always absorbed the people Honolulu needs but declines to house — that is what our walk-ups, our ʻohana units, and our multigenerational households have quietly done for a century. Monster homes are the predatory, unpermitted version of work our neighborhood was already doing lawfully.

The enforcement record

Now the embarrassing half. For years, the standard civil fine for operating a monster home was $250 per day, capped at $2,000.3 Read that again against the business model: a building with fifteen rented bedrooms at even $800 each grosses $12,000 a month. The maximum penalty was less than a week of revenue — not a deterrent, a licensing fee. The Council has since raised the numbers, but the logic of a daily fine hasn’t changed: any penalty that can be priced into the pro forma will be.

And this is not a diffuse problem requiring a thousand prosecutions. When the City Auditor examined monster-home permits in 2019, it traced 78 percent of the plans to just ten developers.4 Ten. A cottage industry with known names, known design professionals, and a repeat-player’s understanding of exactly how little enforcement risk it was carrying.

Meanwhile the remedy that actually bites — revoking the building permit itself — has remained vanishingly rare. When the department revoked the permit for a project on Hanai Loop this year, it was only the fourth revocation in the ordinance’s seven-year life.5 Four revocations, against a decade of construction, against ten firms responsible for most of the plans. That is not a crackdown. That is a rounding error.

Fines are a business expense. Disgorgement is a remedy.

Here is the change in kind, not degree, that I want the Legislature to make: stop metering the violation and start recovering the profit.

State law should authorize — and direct — the counties to disgorge every dollar of rent collected from an unpermitted dwelling unit, from the first tenant to the last, with the recovery secured by a lien on the property.6 Not a fine that accrues while lawyers stall, but restitution of the entire revenue stream the illegal structure produced. A developer can absorb $250 a day forever. No developer builds a fifteen-bedroom rooming house knowing that every rent check is refundable.

Disgorgement also answers the stalling strategy that daily fines invite. Under a fine regime, delay is nearly free. Under disgorgement, delay compounds the liability — every month of continued operation is another month owed back.

The professionals who sign the plans

Every monster home enters the world through a stamped set of plans. An architect or engineer, licensed by the State of Hawaiʻi, certified drawings for a “single-family residence” that both parties understood would operate as nothing of the kind. The contractor who built it holds a state license too.

That is state jurisdiction — my desk, not just the City’s. The licensing boards under the Department of Commerce and Consumer Affairs already have authority to discipline licensees for fraud and repeated misconduct.7 What they have lacked is a pipeline: a requirement that when a county revokes a permit for material misrepresentation, the file is referred to the relevant board automatically, and the board reports back on what it did.

Remember the auditor’s arithmetic — ten developers, 78 percent of the plans. The circle of design professionals serving those ten is smaller still. You do not need a thousand cases to end this industry. You need perhaps a dozen license suspensions, publicly reported, and the stamp — the thing that makes the whole scheme possible — stops being available for hire.

Build the density on purpose

If we stopped every monster home tomorrow, the demand they serve would not vanish. It would move — into converted garages, into overstuffed legal apartments, onto the beaches and into the airport terminal. Enforcement without supply just relocates the shortage.

The honest answer is to legalize the apartment buildings we need, where the infrastructure can carry them — and to say so out loud. The urban core from Kalihi through Kapālama to downtown sits on a rail line we spent billions building. Every station area in our district should be growing lawful homes: real apartment buildings with sprinklers and sewers, missing-middle fourplexes and walk-ups, ʻohana units and ADUs that a working family can actually finance.8

And the upzoning must arrive holding hands with infrastructure money, because the neighbors’ objection about sewer and water mains is frequently correct. Parts of Kalihi run on mains laid generations ago. Monster homes free-ride on that aging capacity — fifteen bathrooms flushing into pipes sized for two. Planned density does the opposite: it comes with capacity built, paid for in part by the development it enables. That is the bargain I will keep putting in front of the Legislature: the state funds the pipes; the counties legalize the homes.

What I’m working on

Three commitments, so you can hold me to them:

  • Disgorgement authority. Legislation empowering counties to recover all rents collected from unpermitted dwelling units, secured by liens, with proceeds funding enforcement staff.
  • Licensing accountability. Automatic referral of permit revocations involving false plans to the DCCA licensing boards, with public reporting of outcomes — starting with the professionals behind the auditor’s ten firms.
  • Infrastructure-paired upzoning. State capital dollars for sewer and water in the urban core, tied to county commitments to legalize multifamily and missing-middle housing around the rail stations our district hosts.

The building on Hanai Loop will not be the last monster home, and one more revocation is not a strategy. The demand for density in urban Honolulu is not going away — it is our children, our parents, our neighbors’ tenants. The only question is whether that density keeps arriving in costume, or whether we finally build it on purpose: lawful, inspected, sprinklered, sewered, and owned by the future instead of extracted from it.
